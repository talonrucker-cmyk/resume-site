Phase 1 · Earning · Stop 1

You’ve been buying it with every paycheck.

A paycheck deduction

6.2% of your wages goes to Social Security (the FICA line); another 1.45% funds Medicare. Your employer quietly matches the Social Security piece dollar-for-dollar.

A cap on the SS part

Only the first ~$183,600 of 2026 wages is taxed for Social Security. Earn above that and the 6.2% stops — but Medicare’s 1.45% never does.

Self-employed pay both halves

No employer to match you, so you owe the full 12.4% for Social Security plus 2.9% for Medicare — the 15.3% self-employment tax.

“It’s not a savings account with your name on it — today’s workers fund today’s retirees, and one day the next generation funds you.”
01 — WHAT IT IS & HOW IT’S FUNDED
Phase 1 · Earning · Stop 2

Ten years of work buys a lifetime benefit.

You earn credits

Work and pay in, and you earn up to 4 credits a year — one for about every $1,800 of 2026 earnings. You max out around $7,240.

40 credits = eligible

Hit 40 credits — about 10 years of work — and you’ve qualified for your own retirement benefit for life. More years don’t add credits, but they can raise the amount.

Family rides along

Once you qualify, a spouse, ex-spouse, or minor kids can draw off your record too — even if they never worked.

02 — DO YOU QUALIFY?
Phase 1 · Earning · Stop 3

Your check is built from your top 35 years.

35 years, indexed

SSA takes your 35 highest-earning years, adjusts old wages for inflation, and averages them into a monthly figure — your AIME.

Fewer years = zeros

Work fewer than 35 years and the missing ones count as $0, dragging the average down. Each extra high-earning year can replace an early low one.

A progressive formula

That average runs through bend points that replace a big share of low earnings and a smaller share of high ones — so lower earners get back a higher percentage.

“Every year you work can knock out a zero or a low year — which is why late-career earnings often move the needle most.”

05 — 62 VS 67 VS 70
Phase 2 · Claiming · Stop 6

Claim early and keep working? Some of it is held back.

Under FRA all year

Earn above about $24,480 in 2026 and SSA withholds $1 for every $2 over — but only while you’re collecting early.

The year you reach FRA

The limit jumps to about $65,160 and the bite eases to $1 for every $3 over — counting only the months before your birthday.

You get it back

Withheld benefits aren’t gone. At FRA, SSA recalculates and pays them back over time as a higher monthly check.

“The earnings test isn’t a tax and isn’t forever — but claiming early while working full-time often just means you gave up the delay bonus for nothing.”
06 — THE EARNINGS TEST
Phase 3 · Family & taxes · Stop 7

A spouse can draw off your record — up to half.

How spousal works

A lower-earning spouse can claim on the other’s record instead of their own.

  • ●Up to 50% of the worker’s FRA benefit — even if the spouse never worked.
  • ●You get the higher of your own or the spousal amount — never both.
  • ●Claim it before your FRA and it’s reduced. Unlike your own, spousal never grows past FRA.
Two people, one work record — and a benefit that shows up whether or not the spouse ever earned a dime.

Divorced? You may still qualify

Married 10+ years and now single? You can claim on an ex-spouse’s record. It doesn’t reduce their benefit or involve them at all.

07 — SPOUSAL BENEFITS
Phase 3 · Family & taxes · Stop 8

Survivor benefits are the hidden reason to delay.

The survivor steps up

When one spouse dies, the survivor keeps the larger of the two checks — not both. The smaller one simply goes away.

Delaying protects them

Every delayed credit the higher earner banks by waiting to 70 carries straight into the survivor’s benefit for life.

As early as 60

A widow or widower can claim survivor benefits as early as 60 (50 if disabled), and switch to their own later if it’s bigger.

“For married couples, the higher earner delaying to 70 isn’t just their bet — it’s a raise for whichever spouse lives longer.”

09 — TAXES ON YOUR BENEFIT
Phase 3 · Family & taxes · Stop 10

At 65, Medicare starts — and it’s tied to your check.

Sign up at 65 — even if you delay SS

Medicare and Social Security are separate clocks. Don’t let one wait on the other.

  • ●Eligibility hits at 65 no matter when you claim SS. Miss the window and you can owe lifelong penalties.
  • ●If you’re already collecting, Part B premiums come straight out of your monthly check.
One clock starts at 65, the other whenever you choose — don’t let a Social Security delay make you miss Medicare.

IRMAA — the high-income surcharge

Higher earners pay more for Part B and D, based on your tax return from two years earlier. One big year — a Roth conversion, a home sale — can quietly raise your premiums later.

10 — MEDICARE & IRMAA
Phase 4 · Big picture · Stop 11

Delaying isn’t a gamble — it’s longevity insurance.

The break-even

Claim early and you get smaller checks sooner; delay and you get bigger checks later. The lines cross in your late 70s to early 80s — live past that and waiting wins.

Insure the risk you can’t afford

The real danger isn’t dying early — it’s outliving your money. The bigger delayed check is guaranteed, inflation-adjusted income that never runs out.

“Claim early if you need the money or have poor health. If you expect a long life, the delayed check is the best-priced insurance you can buy.”
11 — BREAK-EVEN & LONGEVITY
Phase 4 · Big picture · Stop 12

Will it still be there? Mostly, yes.

The honest picture

Social Security’s trust fund is projected to run low around 2033. That’s a shortfall — not a shutdown.

Even then, ~80%

If Congress does nothing, incoming payroll taxes still cover roughly 77–83% of scheduled benefits. A cut, not a zero.

It’s usually patched

Every past shortfall was fixed with small tweaks — a higher cap, a later FRA, adjusted rates. Younger workers should plan for a bit less, not nothing.

“Plan as if you’ll get most of it — and build your own savings so Social Security is the floor, not the whole plan.”
12 — WILL IT STILL BE THERE?
Phase 4 · Big picture · Stop 13

The claim date is a planning decision, not just a form.

Bridge the gap

Want to delay to 70 but retire at 65? Spend from savings for those years to “buy” the bigger, lifelong check.

Convert while it’s cheap

The low-income years before benefits and RMDs start are prime time for Roth conversions — before Social Security fills your brackets.

Coordinate the whole picture

Claim age, taxes, Medicare/IRMAA, and survivor needs all interact. A planner runs them together so one move doesn’t quietly cost you on another.

“The right claim age depends on your savings, your taxes, your health, and your spouse — which is exactly why it’s a plan, not a default.”
13 — FIT IT INTO THE PLAN
The throughline

Three levers, one lifetime check.

Build it

Your earnings record

35 indexed years and 40 credits set the size of your check — and extra working years can replace zeros and raise it.

Time it

Your claim age

Every year you wait from 62 to 70 grows the check — and the survivor’s — by roughly 8%. It’s the biggest lever you control.

Protect it

Taxes, spouse & plan

Manage benefit taxes and IRMAA, coordinate spousal and survivor benefits, and treat Social Security as the floor under your own savings.

You can’t control the program — but the record you build, the age you claim, and the plan around it decide how much of it actually reaches you.
