Phase 1 · Basics · Stop 1

You’re not taxed on what you make.

Start with income

Add up everything you earned — wages, side gigs, interest. Subtract a few adjustments and you get your AGI (adjusted gross income).

Subtract a deduction

Then take the standard deduction (or itemize). What’s left is your taxable income — the only number the tax brackets ever touch.

Fill the brackets

Rates are marginal: your first dollars are taxed low, later dollars higher. A raise into the next bracket only taxes the dollars above the line — never all of it.

“Income − deductions = taxable income. Almost every tax move is just shrinking that middle number.”

02 — STANDARD VS ITEMIZED
Phase 1 · Basics · Stop 3

Sometimes you don’t have to file — but you still should.

The rough line

If your income is under the standard deduction (about $16,100 single in 2026), you often owe nothing and aren’t required to file at all.

File anyway for a refund

If any tax was withheld from a paycheck, filing is the only way to get it back. No return, no refund — the IRS keeps it.

Credits are free money

Low earners often qualify for refundable credits — the EITC, the Child Tax Credit — that can pay you more than you had withheld. You must file to claim them.

“When in doubt, file. Skipping a return you didn’t owe usually just leaves your own money on the table.”

Why the wait pays

Sell before a year and the profit is short-term — taxed like a paycheck, up to 37%. Hold past a year and it’s long-term: just 0%, 15%, or 20%. There is no 22% long-term rate — that’s an ordinary-income bracket.

05 — THE 0% BRACKET
Phase 3 · Working for yourself · Stop 6

Two forms decide who withholds your taxes.

W‑2 — you’re an employee

The classic job. Your employer does the tax work for you.

  • ●Taxes are withheld from every paycheck automatically.
  • ●Your employer pays half of your Social Security & Medicare (7.65%).
  • ●In January you get a W‑2 and mostly just file.

1099 — you’re a contractor

Freelance, gig, or your own business. You’re paid in full — and you’re the boss of the taxes.

  • ●Nothing is withheld — the whole check hits your account.
  • ●You pay both halves of Social Security & Medicare — the 15.3% self-employment tax.
  • ●But you can deduct business expenses a W‑2 worker can’t.
06 — W-2 VS 1099
Phase 3 · Working for yourself · Stop 7

No boss withholds it — so you do.

Set money aside

On 1099 income, park roughly 25–30% of every payment in a separate account the moment it lands. That’s the tax you’ll owe — it was never really yours.

Pay quarterly

The IRS wants it four times a year — April, June, September, January. Skip the estimates and you get hit with penalties on top of the tax.

Draw, don’t “payroll”

As a sole proprietor you don’t run payroll on yourself — you just draw money out. Real payroll only starts if you elect S‑corp (Stop 10).

“The surprise April tax bill isn’t a surprise — it’s the withholding you forgot to do all year.”
07 — PAY YOURSELF & QUARTERLIES
Phase 3 · Working for yourself · Stop 8

Deductions that shrink the business bill.

Business expenses

Anything ordinary and necessary to run the business — software, tools, mileage, a home office — comes off your profit before it’s taxed. Keep the receipts.

Per diem

Travel for work? Instead of saving every meal receipt, you can deduct a flat government per-diem rate for each day away. Simpler, and often larger.

Depreciation

Big assets — a truck, machines, equipment — get deducted over their life. Section 179 and bonus depreciation can let you write off much of it the first year.

“Every legitimate dollar you deduct is a dollar the brackets never get to touch.”
08 — BUSINESS DEDUCTIONS
Phase 3 · Working for yourself · Stop 9

Giving you already do can lower your taxes.

Tithing is a charitable gift

Money given to a church or qualified nonprofit is deductible — but only where it actually helps:

  • 1It counts as an itemized deduction. It only saves tax if your giving plus other itemized items beat the standard deduction.
  • 2New for 2026: even if you take the standard deduction, you can also write off up to $1,000 single / $2,000 joint in cash giving.
Give because you mean to — then make sure the deduction doesn’t slip through the cracks.

Keep the paper trail

Save receipts and year-end giving statements. A steady tither can sometimes “bunch” two years of gifts into one to clear the standard deduction, then itemize that year.

10 — THE S-CORP MOVE
Phase 4 · Structure · Stop 11

When to form an LLC

Once you have something to lose — real revenue, customers, contracts, or assets — an LLC puts a wall between the business and your personal house and savings.

When to hire a CPA

When the stakes beat the fee: self-employment, a business, rentals, equity comp, or a big life change. A good preparer usually finds more than they cost.

11 — ENTITIES & GETTING HELP
The throughline

It all comes back to one number.

Shrink it

Taxable income

Deductions, business expenses, depreciation, giving — every legal move just lowers the income the brackets can reach.

Time it

When you realize

Hold gains past a year, and sell them in low-income years to catch the 0% bracket. Timing changes the rate.

Structure it

How you’re set up

Employee, contractor, LLC, or S‑corp — the wrapper around your income decides which taxes even apply.

You’ll never out-earn a bad tax setup. A few right moves — and a CPA when it counts — keep more of every dollar you make.
